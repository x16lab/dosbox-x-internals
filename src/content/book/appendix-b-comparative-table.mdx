# Appendix B — DOSBox-X in Context: A Comparison with QEMU, 86Box, and PCem {#appendix-b-dosbox-x-in-context-a-comparison-with-qemu-86box-and-pcem}

*Unlike Appendices A, C, and D, this appendix isn't built incrementally from the chapters — it requires understanding three other projects on their own terms, not just DOSBox-X's. What follows is a working draft: the comparison dimensions and the factual claims are checked against available sources, but the connective prose is intentionally left thin until the rest of this book — and DOSBox-X's own design philosophy, as this book has laid it out — is complete enough to compare against with confidence. Treat this as a skeleton to be finished last, not a finished appendix.*

## Why These Three {#why-these-three}

QEMU, 86Box, and PCem cover a useful spread of design philosophies adjacent to DOSBox-X's own, each making a different tradeoff between the same handful of competing goals: hardware fidelity, execution speed, platform scope, and how much of a real machine's BIOS/OS stack the emulator expects you to supply yourself versus reimplements.

- **PCem** — one of the older low-level PC system emulators in this comparison, and the direct ancestor of 86Box (86Box began as a fork of PCem in 2016). PCem emulates real PC hardware down to the chip level and boots real, unmodified BIOS images and operating systems, but has historically made more performance-motivated shortcuts than 86Box, at some cost to accuracy. As of this writing, PCem's original developer has stepped away from the project, with no further versions expected — a useful contrast to DOSBox-X's own multi-contributor, still-active development model covered in @chapter(what-is-dosbox-x).
- **86Box** — forked from PCem specifically to push further on accuracy, particularly for 8088/8086-era systems, at the cost of being more resource-intensive on the host. Like PCem, it's a full system emulator: it interprets real BIOS images and real operating system installs rather than reimplementing DOS services in emulator code, and it tries to track a real machine's actual clock speed and timing rather than DOSBox-X's more approximate `cycles=` model. This is close to DOSBox-X's own goal of high hardware fidelity, but achieved through a categorically different strategy — emulate the real BIOS and let it drive real hardware-accurate boot and OS behavior, rather than DOSBox-X's approach of implementing DOS and much of BIOS behavior directly.
- **QEMU** — a general-purpose machine emulator and virtualizer with a vastly broader scope than any of the other three: it can emulate many CPU architectures, not just x86, and is commonly paired with hardware-assisted virtualization (KVM) to run modern guest operating systems at near-native speed. QEMU's dynamic translation engine (TCG, and multi-threaded TCG) is built for general-purpose speed across arbitrary modern workloads, not for reproducing period-accurate DOS-era hardware quirks — running old DOS-era software under QEMU without period-accurate device emulation tends to expose exactly the kind of timing-dependent bugs @chapter(what-is-dosbox-x) discussed (software that assumed a specific PIT/VGA timing relationship breaking when that relationship no longer holds).

## What This Book Hasn't Yet Established (and Needs to, Before Finishing This Appendix) {#what-this-book-hasn-t-yet-established-and-needs-to-before-finishing-this-appendix}

A few claims above deserve more precision once the relevant chapters exist:

- @chapter(timing-and-cycle-management) (Timing and Cycle Management) needs to fully justify the "approximate cycle budget, not real clock speed" characterization of DOSBox-X against 86Box/PCem's "track real clock speed" approach — right now that's asserted based on general knowledge of both projects, not yet backed by DOSBox-X's own source the way every other claim in this book has been.
- Part IV (BIOS and DOS Kernel chapters) needs to be written before the "implements DOS/BIOS behavior directly" characterization can be stated with the same confidence as the rest of the table.
- It would strengthen this appendix to name one or two concrete pieces of DOS-era software that behave differently across these four emulators specifically *because* of the philosophy differences in the table — a real example does more work than an abstract dimension.
